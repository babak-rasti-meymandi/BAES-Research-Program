### 2. Colleague's Current Resolution Intelligence Position

Colleague describes Resolution Intelligence as a state-observation and state-transition architecture rather than a fully specified enforcement architecture.

His conceptual separation:

- Governance: Who may act?
- Resolution Intelligence: What state is emerging?
- Qualification: Is that state admissible?
- Resolution: What interventions could move the system toward a preferred state?

He currently assigns Resolution Intelligence the following responsibilities:

1. Observe emerging system states.
2. Identify dependencies, constraints, and coordination drift.
3. Qualify whether system states are moving toward or away from desired outcomes.
4. Surface potential resolution paths and their implications.

He explicitly states that the architecture does not independently decide which stakeholder objective should prevail or authorize interventions.

### 3. Hospital Scenario

The Colleague introduced a non-confidential healthcare example involving four stakeholder groups:

- Clinicians: quality of care
- Operations managers: bed availability
- Finance teams: cost control
- Patient-flow coordinators: throughput

Each stakeholder may make individually valid decisions within their respective authority boundaries.

However, interactions among these decisions may produce undesirable collective outcomes:

- Treatment delays
- Discharge bottlenecks
- Increased waiting times
- Reduced system resilience

The central observation:

Individually valid decisions do not necessarily produce desirable collective outcomes.

The user independently recalls having considered a similar hospital scenario previously, although it had not been substantially developed.

### 4. Important Conceptual Distinctions

**A. Human Authority vs. Human Intent**

Human authority may remain a governance invariant while human intent changes, conflicts, or remains incomplete.

**B. Authorization vs. Outcome Quality**

An authorized action may contribute to an undesirable collective state.

An unauthorized action does not become legitimate merely because its outcome appears desirable.

**C. State Observation vs. Authorization**

Observing, evaluating, or predicting a system state does not automatically confer authority to intervene.

**D. Individual Validity vs. Collective Consequences**

Local correctness and global desirability are separate evaluation dimensions.

### 5. Emerging Research Hypothesis

Can individually authorized AI actions collectively produce an undesirable, unsafe, or otherwise unacceptable system-level outcome?

Related research question:

How can system-level state observation complement human-authority governance without becoming an independent source of authorization?

This is an exploratory research hypothesis, not an established BAES requirement or Foundation principle.

### 6. Potential Complementarity

A preliminary conceptual relationship worth investigating:

- Resolution Intelligence: observes interactions, dependencies, emerging states, and potential consequences.
- BAES: addresses human intent, authority, authorization, delegated execution, evidence, decisions, and traceability.

Potential research proposition:

A state-observation capability may provide additional evidence to a governance architecture, while governance determines the legitimate authority and boundaries for subsequent decisions and actions.

No architectural integration has been established.

### 8. Potential Evaluation Matrix

| Action authorization | Collective outcome | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Authorized | Desirable | Authorized and desirable |
| Authorized | Undesirable | Authorized but undesirable |
| Unauthorized | Desirable | Unauthorized despite desirable outcome |
| Unauthorized | Undesirable | Unauthorized and undesirable |

The matrix is a proposed analytical device, not an existing formal BAES model.
